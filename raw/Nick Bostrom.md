**Nick Bostrom** ([/ ˈ b ɒ s t r əm /](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*BOST-rəm*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); [Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_language "Swedish language"): *Niklas Boström* [\[ˈnɪ̌kːlas ˈbûːstrœm\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Swedish "Help:IPA/Swedish"); born 10 March 1973) [^6] is a [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy") known for his work on [existential risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_risk "Existential risk"), the [anthropic principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropic_principle "Anthropic principle"), [human enhancement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_enhancement "Human enhancement") ethics, [whole brain emulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whole_brain_emulation "Whole brain emulation"), [superintelligence risks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_risk_from_artificial_general_intelligence "Existential risk from artificial general intelligence"), and the [reversal test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reversal_test "Reversal test"). He was the founding director of the later defunct [Future of Humanity Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_of_Humanity_Institute "Future of Humanity Institute") at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford") [^7] and has become Principal Researcher at the Macrostrategy Research Initiative.[^8]

Bostrom is the author of *[Anthropic Bias: Observation Selection Effects in Science and Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropic_Bias:_Observation_Selection_Effects_in_Science_and_Philosophy "Anthropic Bias: Observation Selection Effects in Science and Philosophy")* (2002), *[Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence:_Paths,_Dangers,_Strategies "Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies")* (2014) and *Deep Utopia: Life and Meaning in a Solved World* (2024).

Bostrom believes that advances in [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") (AI) may lead to [superintelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence "Superintelligence"), which he defines as "any intellect that greatly exceeds the cognitive performance of humans in virtually all domains of interest". He views this as a major source of opportunities and existential risks.[^7] [^9]

## Early life and education

Born as Niklas Boström in 1973 in [Helsingborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsingborg "Helsingborg"), [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden "Sweden"),[^10] he disliked school at a young age and spent his last year of high school learning from home. He was interested in a wide variety of academic areas, including anthropology, art, literature, and science.[^4]

He received a [B.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") degree from the [University of Gothenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Gothenburg "University of Gothenburg") in 1994.[^11] He then earned an [M.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts "Master of Arts") degree in [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy") and [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") from [Stockholm University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_University "Stockholm University") and an [MSc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Science "Master of Science") degree in [computational neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_neuroscience "Computational neuroscience") from [King's College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King%27s_College_London "King's College London") in 1996. During his time at Stockholm University, he researched the relationship between language and reality by studying the analytic philosopher [W. V. Quine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._V._Quine "W. V. Quine").[^4] He also did some turns on London's stand-up comedy circuit.[^10]

In 2000, he was awarded a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") degree in philosophy from the [London School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics "London School of Economics") under the supervision of [Colin Howson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Howson "Colin Howson") and [Craig Callender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craig_Callender "Craig Callender").[^3] His thesis was titled *Observational selection effects and probability*.[^3] He held a teaching position at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University") from 2000 to 2002, and was a [British Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Academy "British Academy") Postdoctoral Fellow at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford") from 2002 to 2005.[^12]

## Research and writing

### Existential risk

Bostrom's research concerns the future of humanity and long-term outcomes.[^7] [^13] He discusses [existential risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_risk "Existential risk"),[^4] which he defines as one in which an "adverse outcome would either annihilate Earth-originating intelligent life or permanently and drastically curtail its potential". Bostrom is mostly concerned about anthropogenic risks, which are risks arising from human activities, particularly from new technologies such as advanced artificial intelligence, [molecular nanotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_nanotechnology "Molecular nanotechnology"), or [synthetic biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_biology "Synthetic biology").[^14]

In 2005, Bostrom founded the [Future of Humanity Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_of_Humanity_Institute "Future of Humanity Institute") which,[^4] until its shutdown in 2024, researched the [far future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longtermism "Longtermism") of human civilization.[^15] [^16] He is also an adviser to the [Centre for the Study of Existential Risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_for_the_Study_of_Existential_Risk "Centre for the Study of Existential Risk").[^13]

In the 2008 essay collection, *[Global Catastrophic Risks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Catastrophic_Risks_\(book\) "Global Catastrophic Risks (book)")*, editors Bostrom and [Milan M. Ćirković](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milan_M._%C4%86irkovi%C4%87 "Milan M. Ćirković") characterize the relationship between existential risk and the broader class of global catastrophic risks, and link existential risk to [observer selection effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selection_bias#Observer_selection "Selection bias") [^17] and the [Fermi paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_paradox "Fermi paradox").[^18]

#### Vulnerable world hypothesis

In a paper called "The [Vulnerable World Hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulnerable_world_hypothesis "Vulnerable world hypothesis") ",[^19] Bostrom suggests that there may be some technologies that destroy human civilization by default [^1] when discovered. Bostrom proposes a framework for classifying and dealing with these vulnerabilities. He also gives counterfactual thought experiments of how such vulnerabilities could have historically occurred, e.g. if nuclear weapons had been easier to develop or had ignited the atmosphere (as [Edward Teller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Teller "Edward Teller") had feared).[^21]

### Digital sentience

Bostrom supports the substrate independence principle, the idea that consciousness can emerge on various types of physical substrates, not only in "carbon-based biological neural networks" like the human brain.[^22] He considers that " [sentience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentience "Sentience") is a matter of degree" [^23] and that digital minds can in theory be engineered to have a much higher rate and intensity of [subjective experience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subjective_experience "Subjective experience") than humans, using less resources. Such highly sentient machines, which he calls "super-beneficiaries", would be extremely efficient at achieving happiness. He recommends finding "paths that will enable digital minds and biological minds to coexist, in a mutually beneficial way where all of these different forms can flourish and thrive".[^24]

### Anthropic reasoning

Bostrom has published numerous articles on [anthropic reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropic_principle "Anthropic principle"), as well as the book *[Anthropic Bias: Observation Selection Effects in Science and Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropic_Bias:_Observation_Selection_Effects_in_Science_and_Philosophy "Anthropic Bias: Observation Selection Effects in Science and Philosophy")*. In the book, he criticizes previous formulations of the anthropic principle, including those of [Brandon Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandon_Carter "Brandon Carter"), [John Leslie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_A._Leslie "John A. Leslie"), [John Barrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_D._Barrow "John D. Barrow"), and [Frank Tipler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_J._Tipler "Frank J. Tipler").[^25]

Bostrom believes that the mishandling of [indexical information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indexical_information "Indexical information") is a common flaw in many areas of inquiry (including cosmology, philosophy, evolution theory, game theory, and quantum physics). He argues that an anthropic theory is needed to deal with these. He introduces the [self-sampling assumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-Sampling_Assumption "Self-Sampling Assumption") (SSA) and analyzes the [self-indication assumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-Indication_Assumption "Self-Indication Assumption") (SIA), shows how they lead to different conclusions in a number of cases, and identifies how each is affected by paradoxes or counterintuitive implications in certain thought experiments. He argues against SIA and proposes refining SSA into the strong self-sampling assumption (SSSA), which replaces "observers" in the SSA definition with "observer-moments".[^26]

In later work, he proposed with [Milan M. Ćirković](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milan_M._%C4%86irkovi%C4%87 "Milan M. Ćirković") and [Anders Sandberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anders_Sandberg "Anders Sandberg") the phenomenon of *anthropic shadow*, an observation selection effect that prevents observers from observing certain kinds of catastrophes in their recent geological and evolutionary past. They suggest that events that lie in the anthropic shadow are likely to be underestimated unless statistical corrections are made.[^27] [^28]

#### Simulation argument

Bostrom's simulation argument posits that at least one of the following statements is very likely to be true:[^29] [^30]

1. The fraction of human-level civilizations that reach a [posthuman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posthuman "Posthuman") stage is very close to zero;
2. The fraction of posthuman civilizations that are interested in running ancestor-simulations is very close to zero;
3. The fraction of all people with our kind of experiences that are living in a simulation is very close to one.

### Ethics of human enhancement

Bostrom is favorably disposed toward "human enhancement", or "self-improvement and human perfectibility through the ethical application of science", as well as a critic of bio-conservative views.[^31]

In 1998, Bostrom co-founded (with [David Pearce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Pearce_\(philosopher\) "David Pearce (philosopher)")) the World [Transhumanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transhumanism "Transhumanism") Association [^31] (which has since changed its name to [Humanity+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanity%2B "Humanity+")). In 2004, he co-founded (with [James Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hughes_\(sociologist\) "James Hughes (sociologist)")) the [Institute for Ethics and Emerging Technologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Ethics_and_Emerging_Technologies "Institute for Ethics and Emerging Technologies"),[^32] although he is no longer involved with either of these organisations.

In 2005, Bostrom [published](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fable_of_the_Dragon-Tyrant#Publication_history "The Fable of the Dragon-Tyrant") the short story " [The Fable of the Dragon-Tyrant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fable_of_the_Dragon-Tyrant "The Fable of the Dragon-Tyrant") " in the *[Journal of Medical Ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Medical_Ethics "Journal of Medical Ethics")*. A shorter version was published in 2012 in *[Philosophy Now](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_Now "Philosophy Now")*.[^33] The fable personifies death as a dragon that demands a tribute of thousands of people every day. The story explores how [status quo bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_quo_bias "Status quo bias") and learned helplessness can prevent people from taking action to defeat aging even when the means to do so are at their disposal. YouTuber [CGP Grey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CGP_Grey "CGP Grey") created an animated version of the story.[^34]

With philosopher [Toby Ord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toby_Ord "Toby Ord"), he proposed the [reversal test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reversal_test "Reversal test") in 2006. Given humans' irrational status quo bias, how can one distinguish between valid criticisms of proposed changes in a human trait and criticisms merely motivated by resistance to change? The reversal test attempts to do this by asking whether it would be a good thing if the trait was altered in the opposite direction.[^35]

Bostrom's work also considers potential [dysgenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dysgenics "Dysgenics") effects in human populations but he thinks [genetic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_engineering "Genetic engineering") can provide a solution and that "In any case, the time-scale for human natural genetic evolution seems much too grand for such developments to have any significant effect before other developments will have made the issue moot".[^36]

### Technology strategy

Bostrom has suggested that technology policy aimed at reducing existential risk should seek to influence the order in which various technological capabilities are attained, proposing the principle of [differential technological development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_technological_development "Differential technological development"). This principle states that we ought to retard the development of dangerous technologies, particularly ones that raise the level of existential risk, and accelerate the development of beneficial technologies, particularly those that protect against the existential risks posed by nature or by other technologies.[^36]

In 2011, Bostrom founded the Oxford Martin Program on the Impacts of Future Technology.[^37]

Bostrom's theory of the unilateralist's curse has been cited as a reason for the scientific community to avoid controversial dangerous research such as reanimating pathogens.[^38]

### Books

#### Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies

In 2014, Bostrom published *[Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence:_Paths,_Dangers,_Strategies "Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies")*, which became a [*New York Times* Best Seller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times_Best_Seller_list "The New York Times Best Seller list").[^39] The book argues that [superintelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superintelligence "Superintelligence") is possible and explores different types of superintelligences, their cognition, the associated risks. He also presents technical and strategic considerations on how to make it safe.

##### Characteristics of a superintelligence

Bostrom explores multiple possible paths to superintelligence, including [whole brain emulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind_uploading "Mind uploading") and human intelligence enhancement, but focuses on [artificial general intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_general_intelligence "Artificial general intelligence"), explaining that electronic devices have [many advantages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_risk_from_artificial_general_intelligence#Comparison_with_humans "Existential risk from artificial general intelligence") over biological brains.[^40]

Bostrom draws a distinction between [final goals and instrumental goals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_and_intrinsic_value "Instrumental and intrinsic value"). A final goal is what an [agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligent_agent "Intelligent agent") tries to achieve for its own intrinsic value. Instrumental goals are just intermediary steps towards final goals. Bostrom contends there are instrumental goals that will be shared by most sufficiently intelligent agents because they are generally useful to achieve any objective (e.g. preserving the agent's own existence or current goals, acquiring resources, improving its cognition...), this is the concept of [instrumental convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_convergence "Instrumental convergence"). On the other side, he writes that virtually any level of intelligence can in theory be combined with virtually any final goal (even absurd final goals, e.g. [making paperclips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_convergence#Paperclip_maximizer "Instrumental convergence")), a concept he calls the [orthogonality thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_risk_from_artificial_general_intelligence#Orthogonality_thesis "Existential risk from artificial general intelligence").[^41]

He argues that an AI with the ability to improve itself might initiate an [intelligence explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_explosion "Intelligence explosion"), resulting (potentially rapidly) in a superintelligence.[^42] Such a superintelligence could have vastly superior capabilities, notably in strategizing, social manipulation, hacking or economic productivity. With such capabilities, a superintelligence could outwit humans and take over the world, establishing a [singleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_\(global_governance\) "Singleton (global governance)") (which is "a world order in which there is at the global level a single decision-making agency" [^2]) and optimizing the world according to its final goals.[^40]

Bostrom argues that giving simplistic final goals to a superintelligence could be catastrophic:

> Suppose we give an A.I. the goal to make humans smile. When the A.I. is weak, it performs useful or amusing actions that cause its user to smile. When the A.I. becomes superintelligent, it realizes that there is a more effective way to achieve this goal: take control of the world and stick electrodes into the facial muscles of humans to cause constant, beaming grins.[^36]

##### Mitigating the risk

Bostrom explores several pathways to reduce the [existential risk from AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_risk_from_artificial_general_intelligence "Existential risk from artificial general intelligence"). He emphasizes the importance of international collaboration, notably to reduce [race to the bottom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_to_the_bottom "Race to the bottom") and [AI arms race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_arms_race "Artificial intelligence arms race") dynamics. He suggests potential techniques to help control AI, including containment, stunting AI capabilities or knowledge, narrowing the operating context (e.g. to question-answering), or "tripwires" (diagnostic mechanisms that can lead to a shutdown).[^40] But Bostrom contends that "we should not be confident in our ability to keep a superintelligent genie locked up in its bottle forever. Sooner or later, it will out". He thus suggests that in order to be safe for humanity, superintelligence must be [aligned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_alignment "AI alignment") with morality or human values so that it is "fundamentally on our side".[^43] Potential AI [normativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normativity "Normativity") frameworks include [Yudkowsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eliezer_Yudkowsky "Eliezer Yudkowsky") 's [coherent extrapolated volition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherent_extrapolated_volition "Coherent extrapolated volition") (human values improved via extrapolation), [moral rightness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality "Morality") (doing what is morally right), and moral permissibility (following humanity's coherent extrapolated volition except when it's morally impermissible).[^40]

Bostrom warns that an existential catastrophe can also occur from AI being misused by humans for destructive purposes, or from humans failing to take into account the potential moral status of digital minds. Despite these risks, he says that machine superintelligence seems involved at some point in "all the plausible paths to a really great future".[^9]

##### Public reception

The book became a [*New York Times* Best Seller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times_Best_Seller_list "The New York Times Best Seller list") and received positive feedback from personalities such as [Stephen Hawking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hawking "Stephen Hawking"), [Bill Gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Gates "Bill Gates"), [Elon Musk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elon_Musk "Elon Musk"), [Peter Singer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Singer "Peter Singer") and [Derek Parfit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Parfit "Derek Parfit"). It was praised for offering clear and compelling arguments on a neglected yet important topic. It was sometimes criticized for spreading pessimism about the potential of AI, or for focusing on longterm and speculative risks.[^44] Some skeptics such as [Daniel Dennett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Dennett "Daniel Dennett") or [Oren Etzioni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oren_Etzioni "Oren Etzioni") contended that superintelligence is too far away for the risk to be significant.[^45] [^46] [Yann LeCun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yann_LeCun "Yann LeCun") considers that there is no existential risk, asserting that superintelligent AI will have no desire for self-preservation [^47] and that experts can be trusted to make it safe.[^48]

[Raffi Khatchadourian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raffi_Khatchadourian "Raffi Khatchadourian") wrote that Bostrom's book on superintelligence "is not intended as a treatise of deep originality; Bostrom's contribution is to impose the rigors of [analytic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_philosophy "Analytic philosophy") on a messy corpus of ideas that emerged at the margins of academic thought." [^44]

#### Deep Utopia: Life and Meaning in a Solved World

In his 2024 book, *Deep Utopia: Life and Meaning in a Solved World*, Bostrom explores the concept of an ideal life, if humanity transitions successfully into a post-superintelligence world. Bostrom notes that the question is "not how interesting a future is to look at, but how good it is to live in." He outlines some technologies that he considers physically possible in theory and available at technological maturity, such as [cognitive enhancement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_enhancement "Cognitive enhancement"), [reversal of aging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reversal_of_aging "Reversal of aging"), arbitrary sensory inputs (taste, sound...), or the precise control of motivation, mood, well-being and personality. According to him, not only machines would be better than humans at working, but they would also undermine the purpose of many leisure activities, providing extreme welfare while challenging the quest for meaning.[^49] [^50]

## Public engagement

Bostrom has provided policy advice and consulted for many governments and organizations. He gave evidence to the [House of Lords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Lords "House of Lords"), Select Committee on Digital Skills.[^51] He is an advisory board member for the [Machine Intelligence Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_Intelligence_Research_Institute "Machine Intelligence Research Institute"),[^52] [Future of Life Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_of_Life_Institute "Future of Life Institute"),[^53] and an external advisor for the Cambridge [Centre for the Study of Existential Risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_for_the_Study_of_Existential_Risk "Centre for the Study of Existential Risk").[^54]

### 1996 email incident

In January 2023, Bostrom issued an apology [^55] for a 1996 [listserv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LISTSERV "LISTSERV") email [^56] he sent as a postgrad where he had stated that he thought "Blacks are more stupid than whites", and where he also used the word " [niggers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigger "Nigger") " in a description of how he thought this statement might be perceived by others.[^57] [^58] The apology, posted on his website,[^55] stated that "the invocation of a racial slur was repulsive" and that he "completely repudiate\[d\] this disgusting email".[^57]

The email has been described as "racist" in several news sources.[^59] [^60] [^61] [^57] According to [Andrew Anthony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Anthony "Andrew Anthony") of *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian "The Guardian")*, "The apology did little to placate Bostrom’s critics, not least because he conspicuously failed to withdraw his central contention regarding [race and intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_and_intelligence "Race and intelligence"), and seemed to make a partial defence of [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics "Eugenics")." [^62]

Shortly afterward, Oxford University condemned the language used in the email and started an investigation.[^57] [^60] The investigation concluded on 10 August 2023: "\[W\]e do not consider you to be a racist or that you hold racist views, and we consider that the apology you posted in January 2023 was sincere." [^63] [^62]

## Personal life

Bostrom met his wife Susan in 2002. As of 2015, she lived in [Montreal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal "Montreal") and Bostrom in Oxford. They have one son.[^4]
