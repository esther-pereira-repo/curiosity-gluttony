***The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind*** is a 1976 book by the Princeton psychologist, psychohistorian [^1] and [consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness "Consciousness") theorist [Julian Jaynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Jaynes "Julian Jaynes") (1920–1997). It explores the nature of consciousness – particularly "the ability to introspect" – and its evolution in ancient human history. Jaynes proposes that consciousness is a [learned behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learned_behavior "Learned behavior") rooted in language and culture rather than being innate. He distinguishes consciousness from [sensory awareness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awareness "Awareness") and [cognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognition "Cognition"). Jaynes introduces the concept of the " [bicameral mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameral_mentality "Bicameral mentality") ", a non-conscious mentality prevalent in early humans that relied on [auditory hallucinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auditory_hallucination "Auditory hallucination").

In his book, Jaynes examines historical texts and archaeological evidence to support his theory. He places the origin of consciousness around the [2nd millennium BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_millennium_BC "2nd millennium BC") and suggests that the transition from the bicameral mind to consciousness was triggered by the breakdown of the bicameral system. The bicameral mind, he explains, was characterized by individuals experiencing auditory hallucinations as commands from gods, guiding their actions.

The book gained attention and was well-received upon its release.[^2] [^3] [^4] It generated several positive book reviews, including mentions by notable critics such as [John Updike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Updike "John Updike") and [Christopher Lehmann-Haupt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Lehmann-Haupt "Christopher Lehmann-Haupt"). The theory proposed by Jaynes influenced philosophers like [Daniel Dennett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Dennett "Daniel Dennett") and [Susan Blackmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Blackmore "Susan Blackmore"), as well as researchers studying [schizophrenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schizophrenia "Schizophrenia"). Jaynes's ideas on consciousness and the bicameral mind have been explored in various conferences, publications, and discussions over the years.[^5]

In addition to numerous reviews and commentaries, there are several summaries of the book's material, for example, in the journal *[Behavioral and Brain Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_and_Brain_Sciences "Behavioral and Brain Sciences")*, and in lectures and discussions published in *[Canadian Psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Psychology "Canadian Psychology")*.[^5] It was Jaynes's only book, and is still in print in several languages.

## Bicameral mind

In this book, Jaynes reviews what one of his early critics acknowledged as the "spectacular history of failure" [^6] to explain [consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness "Consciousness") – "the human ability to introspect".[^7] Abandoning the assumption that consciousness is innate, Jaynes explains it instead as a learned behavior that "arises \[...\] from language, and specifically from [metaphor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_metaphor "Conceptual metaphor")." [^6] With this understanding, Jaynes then demonstrated that ancient texts and archeology can reveal a history of human mentality alongside the histories of other cultural products. His analysis of the evidence led him not only to place the origin of consciousness during the 2nd millennium BC but also to hypothesize the existence of an older non-conscious "mentality that he called the bicameral mind, referring to the brain's two hemispheres".[^8]

## Contents

The book is divided into three parts, designated as "Books". Book I, titled "The Mind of Man", discusses consciousness, and raises the question of precisely when in history man may have become conscious of his own consciousness. It makes observations about Homer's *Iliad*, and proposes the hypothesis of a [bicameral mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameral_mind "Bicameral mind"), linking it to the origin of civilization.

Book II, titled "The Witness of History", applied this hypothesis to [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic "Neolithic") culture and the rise of the Mesopotamian and Greek civilizations. Book III, titled "Vestiges of the Bicameral Mind in the Modern World", applies the hypothesis to modern psychological theories of authority, prophecy, possession, poetry, music, hypnosis, and schizophrenia.

## Reception and influence

*The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind* was a successful work of popular science, selling out the first print run before a second could replace it.[^9] It received some positive book reviews, such as those by [John Updike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Updike "John Updike") in *The New Yorker*, [Christopher Lehmann-Haupt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Lehmann-Haupt "Christopher Lehmann-Haupt") in the *New York Times*,[^10] and [Marshall McLuhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_McLuhan "Marshall McLuhan") in the Toronto *Globe and Mail*. Articles on Jaynes and his ideas appeared in *Time* [^2] and *[Psychology Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology_Today "Psychology Today")* [^3] in 1977, and in *Quest/78* in 1978.[^4] The book was nominated for the [National Book Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Book_Award "National Book Award") in Contemporary Thought in 1978.[^11]

A new edition, with an afterword that addressed some criticisms and restated the main themes, was published in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") in 1990 and in the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom "United Kingdom") (by Penguin Books) in 1993,[^12] and was re-issued in 2000.[^13]

The book is mentioned in [Richard Dawkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dawkins "Richard Dawkins") 's 2006 work *[The God Delusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_God_Delusion "The God Delusion")* as "one of those books that is either complete rubbish or a work of consummate genius, nothing in between! Probably the former, but I'm hedging my bets." Jaynes's work on consciousness has influenced philosophers [Daniel Dennett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Dennett "Daniel Dennett"),[^14] [Susan Blackmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Blackmore "Susan Blackmore"), and [Ken Wilber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Wilber "Ken Wilber"), and the bicameral model of the [cerebral hemispheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerebral_hemisphere "Cerebral hemisphere") has influenced schizophrenia researchers Henry Nasrallah [^15] and [Tim Crow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Crow "Tim Crow").[^16]

The theory of bicamerality inspired early investigations of [auditory hallucination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auditory_hallucination "Auditory hallucination") by psychologist Thomas Posey [^17] and clinical psychologist John Hamilton.[^18] With further research in the late 1990s using new brain imaging technology, Jaynes's ideas have received renewed attention [^19] [^20] and recognition for contributing to a rethinking of auditory hallucinations and mental illness.[^21]

Jaynes described the range of responses to his book as "from people who feel \[the ideas are\] very important all the way to very strong hostility.... When someone comes along and says consciousness is in history, it can't be accepted. If \[psychologists\] did accept it, they wouldn't have the motivation to go back into the laboratory..." [^4]

Marcel Kuijsten, founder of the [Julian Jaynes Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Jaynes_Society "Julian Jaynes Society"), wrote that in the decades since the book's publication, "there have been few in-depth discussions, either positive or negative" about it, rejecting as too simplistic the criticism that "Jaynes was wrong." [^8]
