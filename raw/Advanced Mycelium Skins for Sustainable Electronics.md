## Abstract

Developing sustainable substrates for electronics is key to reduce the global reliance on fossil-based synthetic polymers. Mycelium-based materials are emerging as promising alternatives as they up-cycle agricultural wastes and offer biodegradability. However, their often inhomogeneous growth and relatively rough surface limit their applicability, especially for thin-film electronics. Here, post-growth treatments such as deacetylation of chitin that modify the structure and composition of the mycelium and improve its mechanical and electrical characteristics are introduced. The resulting mycelium foil demonstrates extended durability, reduced electrical conductivity, and a reduced surface roughness *R <sub>rms</sub>* of 2.7 ± 0.7 µm while maintaining its thermal stability and biodegradability. Metallic thin films deposited onto these foils exhibit conductivities as high as 18.08 ± 2.38 × 10 <sup>4</sup> Scm <sup>−1</sup> and withstand more than 10000 bending repetitions. Coating the mycelium foil with biodegradable shellac further smoothens its surface and provides moisture resistance. In addition, it serves as an adhesive for the lamination of copper (Cu) layers which, structured by laser ablation, enable flexible electronic devices such as a Near-Field-Communication-Tag. The recovery of non-biodegradable components is moreover enabled through triggered dissolution in ethanol. These developments in mycelium-based electronics offer viable solutions to promote eco-friendly manufacturing processes and ultimately reduce electronic waste.

## 1 Introduction

In the endeavor to create new, highly functional, yet cost-effective materials, we increasingly rely on petroleum-based plastics despite their negative environmental impact.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0001" aria-label="Reference 1 - J. Toxicol. Risk Assess.">1</a></sup><sup>]</sup> In 2022, the global plastic production reached over 400 Mt, of which only 8.9% were recycled and 0.5% were bio-based.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0002" aria-label="Reference 2">2</a></sup><sup>]</sup>

Although widely used, the remnants of such polymer-based products are hardly biodegradable, and their inadequate disposal leads to plastic waste accumulation in landfills or the environment. Consequently, substituting these materials with biodegradable equivalents would significantly improve our ecological footprint.

Mycelium-based materials, whether pure or in composites, have emerged as promising candidates in this respect due to their desirable and tunable mechanical properties resulting from their morphology. Especially the low-cost, facile, and mostly carbon-neutral fabrication using either liquid or solid-state fermentation facilitates their proliferation in research and industry.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0003" aria-label="Reference 3 - Trends Biotechnol.">3</a>, <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0004" aria-label="Reference 4 - Recent Adv. White Biotechnol. Fungi">4</a></sup><sup>]</sup> Here, the possibility of up-cycling of abundant agricultural by-products like coffee grounds, wood shavings or straw as a growing medium adds another layer of sustainability.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0005" aria-label="Reference 5 - Waste Manag.">5</a>, <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0006" aria-label="Reference 6 - Appl. Microbiol. Biotechnol.">6</a></sup><sup>]</sup> Post-processing of the as-grown mycelium mats in the form of physical (pressing, drying, mechanical abrasion,…) or chemical treatments (deproteination, deacytelation, cross-linking, dyeing,…) noticeably promote their mechanical and optical properties.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0007" aria-label="Reference 7 - Nat. Sustain.">7</a></sup><sup>]</sup> Ultimately, this has led to the widespread adoption of mycological biomaterials in sectors such as paper,<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0008" aria-label="Reference 8 - React. Funct. Polym.">8</a>, <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0009" aria-label="Reference 9 - Biomacromolecules">9</a></sup><sup>]</sup> textiles,<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0010" aria-label="Reference 10">10</a>, <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0011" aria-label="Reference 11">11</a></sup><sup>]</sup> wound management,<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0012" aria-label="Reference 12 - Mar. Drugs">12</a> - <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0014" aria-label="Reference 14 - J. Biomed. Mater. Res.">14</a></sup><sup>]</sup> leather substitutes,<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0003" aria-label="Reference 3 - Trends Biotechnol.">3</a>, <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0007" aria-label="Reference 7 - Nat. Sustain.">7</a></sup><sup>]</sup> construction materials <sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0015" aria-label="Reference 15 - Mater. Des.">15</a> - <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0017" aria-label="Reference 17 - Int. J. Biomater.">17</a></sup><sup>]</sup> and many others. For example, the natural foam-like structure of mycelium mats, along with their mechanical stability and lightweight nature, makes them well-suited as a replacement for commodity plastics like polystyrene in the packaging industry.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0018" aria-label="Reference 18 - Mater. Today: Proc.">18</a></sup><sup>]</sup> When inoculated in agar-based hydrogels, mycelia are even 3D-printable and can assemble into complex structures with self-healing properties.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0019" aria-label="Reference 19 - Adv. Funct. Mater.">19</a>, <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0020" aria-label="Reference 20 - Mater. Des.">20</a></sup><sup>]</sup> However, the most advanced and industrialized application of fungal materials are bovine leather substitutes, which considerably lower the environmental impact compared to their synthetic equivalents polyvinyl chloride and polyurethane.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0007" aria-label="Reference 7 - Nat. Sustain.">7</a></sup><sup>]</sup> Here, processing of harvested mycelium mats includes chemical treatments such as solvents, and alkaline or acidic solutions to remove soluble components like proteins or deacetylate chitin, which opens sites for cross-linking.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0010" aria-label="Reference 10">10</a></sup><sup>]</sup> The subsequently pressed and dried material exhibits increased density, strength, and elasticity.

Going beyond their use in construction or clothing, the unique properties of mycelial materials render them highly promising for flexible and biodegradable electronics.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0021" aria-label="Reference 21 - Adv. Funct. Mater.">21</a> - <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0027" aria-label="Reference 27 - Front. Electron.">27</a></sup><sup>]</sup> We recently reported their implementation as substrate for flexible printed circuit boards (PCBs) and as a separator in batteries.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0028" aria-label="Reference 28 - Sci. Adv.">28</a></sup><sup>]</sup> This is enabled by the morphology of Ganoderma lucidum, a red-colored fungi that naturally grows on decaying hardwood, in the form of premature as-grown mycelium skins that were only dried and mechanically pressed. Although the natural microporous structure of mycelium skins fosters ion transport, its high surface roughness and subpar mechanical and electrical properties can be detrimental when used as a substrate for thin-film electronics. Integrating structural modifications through post-harvest chemical treatments into our functionalized mycelium-based substrates therefore offers a viable route for significantly improving their applicability without compromising sustainability.

We herein propose a new generation of mycelium-based electronics utilizing environmentally friendly chemical treatments of pre-mature Ganoderma lucidum skins to enhance their applicability as PCB substrates (**Figure** [1](#adfm202412196-fig-0001)). We investigate the change in Young's modulus and ultimate stress and strain of pristine mycelium compared to samples treated with sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and acetic acid (AcOH). Herein proposed treatments cause deacetylation of the cellular chitin and the removal of the resulting chitosan, which ultimately leads to a smoothed surface structure. Conveniently, the extracted chitosan is a valuable bio-polymer that can be further purified and utilized in biomedicine and in the food industry.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0029" aria-label="Reference 29 - Int. J. Mol. Sci.">29</a> - <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0031" aria-label="Reference 31 - Biomed. Pharmacother.">31</a></sup><sup>]</sup> Our modification processes result in thin translucent mycelium foils with low surface roughness, excellent thermal stability, and improved electrical and mechanical properties. Despite the chemically induced structural changes, the mycelium skins remain entirely biodegradable as verified through composting tests. We use physical vapor deposition (PVD) and laser ablation to create metal paths on the mycelium foil with enhanced durability and conductivity. Furthermore, utilizing biodegradable shellac as a coating or adhesive layer enables a recycling concept to recover non-biodegradable materials from mycelium-based substrates in thin-film electronics and PCBs. This way, the recovered substrates can be reused or composted to degrade into benign components separately. Finally, we demonstrate a flexible PCB incorporating near-field-communication (NFC) by lamination and laser ablation of a copper film on the mycelium substrate. With these advances, our mycelium-based electronics bring us one step closer to a sustainable yet industrially adaptable alternative for conventional PCBs.

## 2 Results

### 2.1 Cultivating and Chemical Treatment of Mycelium Skin

Mycelium skin was cultivated by solid-state fermentation of Ganoderma lucidum with beechwood-based growing medium. A fine polyethylene (PE) grid placed on top of the inoculated substrate serves as a separator to obtain a pure fungal material (**Figure** [**2** a](#adfm202412196-fig-0002)). It is harvested in the early growth phase, typically after two weeks, to ensure a homogeneously covered surface without the development of fruiting bodies or a crust. Although this material has electrical properties sufficient for the use as a PCB substrate, its surface structure entails adverse effects to applied conductive materials. Fungal mycelium, a complex bio-material with numerous constituents, can however change significantly when exposed to various chemicals. Leveraging chemical treatments akin to the fabrication of leather substitutes allows us to modify the underlying hyphae structure, especially the fungal cell wall, which is mainly composed of chitin, glucans, and proteins.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0032" aria-label="Reference 32 - Int. J. Microbiol.">32</a></sup><sup>]</sup>

Flexible substrates for electronic circuit boards must exhibit a certain degree of elasticity to promote their bendability, yet still have a sufficiently high Young's modulus and high ultimate stress so that the material is resistant to deformation or rupture under handling related stress. Therefore, we exposed pristine mycelium skin samples to deionized (DI) water, sulfuric acid (H <sub>2</sub> SO <sub>4</sub>), hydrochloric acid (HCl), AcOH, and NaOH, (detailed description in Experimental Section) and investigated weight change and resulting mechanical properties. Dog bone-shaped specimens were weighed before and after being placed in a flask filled with the corresponding solution (Figure [2b](#adfm202412196-fig-0002); Table [S1](#support-information-section), Supporting Information). Uniaxial tensile tests were then carried out to determine the ultimate stress, ultimate strain, and Young's modulus (Figure [2c,d](#adfm202412196-fig-0002)).

DI water leads to a weight loss of 16% and a doubling of Young's modulus and ultimate stress to 293 ± 51 and 3.86 ± 1.31 MPa respectively, which is most likely due to the removal of water-soluble polysaccharides.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0033" aria-label="Reference 33 - Food Bioprod. Process.">33</a>, <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0034" aria-label="Reference 34 - Food Hydrocolloids">34</a></sup><sup>]</sup> In comparison, exposure to 10% AcOH has no additional discernible effect on mechanical properties or weight loss. However, stronger acids such as HCl and H <sub>2</sub> SO <sub>4</sub> dissolve the material over time and no further investigations were possible. Using a solution of 2% NaOH, an alkali reported to deproteinate the mycelium,<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0035" aria-label="Reference 35 - Food Chem.">35</a></sup><sup>]</sup> results in a weight loss of 39.1% and a significantly increased Young's modulus of 656 ± 162 MPa. Simultaneously, ultimate stress increased to 12.34 ± 6.41 MPa while ultimate strain decreased to 2.4% ± 1.5%. A much higher concentration of 50% reduces the initial weight by 56.1%. Thus, the Young's modulus decreases marginally, the ultimate stress doubles to 21.77 ± 6.10 MPa and the ultimate strain increases significantly to 6.5 ± 3.2%, which indicates structural changes in the material. These are most likely caused by alkali deacetylation using NaOH that transforms chitin, a biopolymer that provides structural stability to the fungal cell walls, into chitosan. However, this polymer is less rigid and the process therefore weakens the hyphae network.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0032" aria-label="Reference 32 - Int. J. Microbiol.">32</a>, <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0036" aria-label="Reference 36 - Prog. Polym. Sci.">36</a>, <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0037" aria-label="Reference 37 - Nat. Rev. Microbiol.">37</a></sup><sup>]</sup> Combining the previous two steps, 2% NaOH followed by 50% NaOH, the Young's Modulus decreases even further in comparison to the 50% NaOH treatment. Interestingly, the overall weight loss of 41.9% is close to the single 2% NaOH treatment, which indicates an inhibited extraction rate for a subsequent 50% NaOH treatment. To exclude measurement errors, we repeated this part of the experiment and found consistent results (Figure [S3](#support-information-section), Supporting Information).

Finally, adding a subsequent 10% AcOH treatment yields the best overall results. In this step chitosan is removed from the mycelium,<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0035" aria-label="Reference 35 - Food Chem.">35</a></sup><sup>]</sup> leading to an additional weight loss of 2.7%. The resulting material becomes translucent, outperforms pristine samples in ultimate stress and strain with values of 24.55 ± 5.32 MPa and 7.5 ± 2.6% respectively, and features a high Young's modulus of 556 ± 127 MPa and a decreased thickness from 110 ± 24 to 60 ± 10 µm. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and profilometry corroborate our assumption of a smoothed surface (Figure [2e](#adfm202412196-fig-0002)) with a reduction in roughness *R* <sub>rms</sub> from 7.5 ± 1.8 to 2.7 ± 0.7 µm (1 mm scanning length, Figure [2f](#adfm202412196-fig-0002)) after the combined chemical processing steps. The combination of 2% and 50% NaOH, followed by 10% AcOH, leads to severe structural changes, and ultimately to a collapsing hyphae network, thus smoothing the surface and additionally hindering the light dispersion through the material. The advantages of a smoother surface are particularly evident in thin-film electronics, where irregularities are transmitted through all the layers applied and thus impair functionality. Therefore, lower surface roughness allows for thinner layers with higher conductivity as well as the application of finer structures. In the following, we will refer to this modified mycelium as mycelium foil.

The manufacturing of PCBs typically implies exposure to high temperatures, e.g. during soldering. Affected materials thus must not exhibit temperature-induced changes of their structure to ensure compatibility with PCB manufacturing. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) reveals negligible changes to thermal stability up to temperatures of 250 °C, which is comparable to pristine mycelium skin and sufficient to allow the soldering of electrical components (Figure [2g](#adfm202412196-fig-0002)).

Furthermore, a substrate's suitability is determined by its electrical properties, which are sought to maximize possible applications and exclude complications and impairments during operation. Hereby, the conductivity *σ* and the loss tangent *δ* of the material are crucial characteristics that should be as low as possible in order to minimize leaking currents and reduce unnecessary power losses. In comparison to our preceding work,<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0028" aria-label="Reference 28 - Sci. Adv.">28</a></sup><sup>]</sup> the conductivity *σ* decreases by two orders of magnitude to 1.72 ± 0.31 × 10 <sup>−11</sup> Scm <sup>−1</sup> at 10 mHz (Figure [S2](#support-information-section), Supporting Information), which is similar to unprocessed paper with ≈10 <sup>−10</sup> to 10 <sup>−14</sup> Scm <sup>−1</sup>.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0038" aria-label="Reference 38 - J. Imaging Sci. Technol.">38</a>, <a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0039" aria-label="Reference 39 - Adv. Mater.">39</a></sup><sup>]</sup> At the same time, the relative permittivity *ε <sub>r</sub>* stays unchanged at 2.75 ± 0.60 at 1 MHz, while the loss tangent *δ* almost halved to 0.035 ± 0.002 at 1 MHz. Finally, the dielectric strength of 20.6 ± 4.9 kVmm <sup>−1</sup>, surpasses the one of untreated mycelium by over 50% and is comparable to FR-4,<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0040" aria-label="Reference 40 - IEEE Trans. Appl. Supercond.">40</a></sup><sup>]</sup> a common material class employed in rigid PCBs. Ultimately, optimizing the mechanical properties of our material, in particular the surface roughness, and improving the electrical properties are a decisive step on the way to broad application.

As we introduce processing steps that alter the material substantially, unchanged sustainability in terms of residue-free biodegradation of the mycelium foil must still be assured after the removal of mounted electronic components. While the mycelium is durable under ambient conditions, it readily degrades when introduced in an environment that features specialized microorganisms and enzymes. Therefore, we examined the aerobic disintegration of mycelium foil mixed with humidified composting soil. Within only ten days, it loses 94.5% of its initial mass (Figure [2h,i](#adfm202412196-fig-0002)). At this point, remnants are getting too small and indistinguishable from the soil to allow continuation of the experiment.

### 2.2 Mycelium Foil as Substrate for Electronics

Caused by the collapse of the hyphae network, the processed mycelium now exhibits a smooth surface that enhances the continuity and processability of thin-film electronics (**Figure** [**3** a](#adfm202412196-fig-0003)). Consequently, metallic films created with PVD have an increased sheet conductivity and finely laser-ablated structures are less prone to local defects.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0023" aria-label="Reference 23 - Adv. Funct. Mater.">23</a></sup><sup>]</sup> Therefore, to test the conductance of metallic films, 200 nm of copper was deposited onto the mycelium foil with a 3 nm chromium (Cr) pre-deposition as an adhesion layer.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0041" aria-label="Reference 41 - ACS Nano">41</a></sup><sup>]</sup> This chromium layer prevents the delamination of the overlying copper film during soldering (Figure [S3](#support-information-section), Supporting Information). We achieved a reproducible trace conductivity of 18.08 ± 2.38 × 10 <sup>4</sup> Scm <sup>−1</sup>, which exceeds our preceding work by a factor of two.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0028" aria-label="Reference 28 - Sci. Adv.">28</a></sup><sup>]</sup> After cyclic bending through 10000 repetitions with radii between 22.5 and 5 mm, the resistance increases by only 7.5%, which showcases the mechanical reliability of the traces (Figure [3b](#adfm202412196-fig-0003)). The observed reduction in conductivity is due to the formation of microcracks within the metal coating (Figure [S4](#support-information-section), Supporting Information).<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0042" aria-label="Reference 42 - Adv. Mater.">42</a></sup><sup>]</sup>

With its high flexibility and durability, our mycelium foil proves highly applicable as a substrate for thin-film electronics. However, the foil remains a hygroscopic material with ionic conductance and is still prone to changes induced by the ambient humidity that affects its electrical properties and the corrosion behavior of the applied thin-film electronics. This can be counteracted by a coating made from a hydrophobic material like shellac, a biodegradable thermoplastic resin. It is applied by airbrushing an alcoholic solution, creating a thin layer that impedes water uptake from the environment (Figure [3a](#adfm202412196-fig-0003)). Coated samples have 50%–70% less water absorption than pristine samples (Figure [S5](#support-information-section), Supporting Information). Other positive effects are the higher electrical insulation of the mycelium <sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0043" aria-label="Reference 43 - Green Chem.">43</a></sup><sup>]</sup> and a further reduced surface roughness *R <sub>rms</sub>* of 1.29 ± 0.61 µm (1 mm scanning length, Figure [3c](#adfm202412196-fig-0003)). Consequently, this allows metal depositions with a trace conductivity of 36.33 ± 2.22 × 10 <sup>4</sup> Scm <sup>−1</sup>, two times higher than without coating. In addition, the smoothness of the surface allows the fabrication of reliable conductive paths with a width of less than 300 µm for laser-ablateded structures and less than 150 µm for structures deposited with PVD through a mask (Figure [3d](#adfm202412196-fig-0003)).

Even for applications with high currents, the traces can sustain current densities up to 1.25 kAmm <sup>‒2</sup> before the shellac coating melts and the connection is interrupted (Figure [S6](#support-information-section), Supporting Information). This is caused by its low melting point of ≈75–80 °C in combination with the low thickness of the deposited metal layer.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0044" aria-label="Reference 44 - Trends Food Sci. Technol.">44</a></sup><sup>]</sup>

Furthermore, the use of shellac adds a desirable feature, as its solubility in organic solvents enables a feasible recycling routine for mycelium-based PCBs. We showcase this with a meander-shaped strain sensor, structured via laser ablation of metal deposited on the coating, first using pristine mycelium foil then on a recycled one as a substrate (Figure [3e](#adfm202412196-fig-0003)). Immersing this sensor in ethanol for 5–10 min dissolves the shellac layer and thus detaches the metal, which can then be recovered. Afterward, the substrate is ready to be reused or composted without any adverse effects (Figure [3f](#adfm202412196-fig-0003)). We investigated such a recycled sensor with cyclic tensile tests in which we measured the normalized resistance change during a 50-fold elongation between 0.5% and 3.5% (Figure [3g](#adfm202412196-fig-0003)). The metallic deposition was past its mechanical limit and the sensors resistance increased over each cycle, whereas the mycelium foil endured the mechanical stress without any visible damages proving the reliable usability of our material.

Inspired by traditional PCB manufacturing, the protective coating of shellac can be used as adhesive for the lamination of thin copper foils (10 µm) onto our mycelium foil (**Figure** [**4** a](#adfm202412196-fig-0004)). Even after extensive laser ablation, due the 50 times thicker metal layer, the mycelium stays intact and the remaining copper parts still adhere reliably to it (Figure [4b](#adfm202412196-fig-0004)). Irrespective of the different processing, immersing the board for several minutes in ethanol again dissolves the shellac and thus enables recycling of metal and substrate (Figure [4c](#adfm202412196-fig-0004)).

Utilizing the main advantage of this process, a robust copper layer, it is now possible to reliably solder electronic components onto the circuit board. Although the shellac underneath still melts, the continuity of the metal is no longer affected. We hereby demonstrate the fabrication of an NFC-tag on our mycelium substrate (Figure [4d](#adfm202412196-fig-0004)). Therefore, we engraved a 4.85 mH copper coil for energy and data transmission into the laminated copper layer and hand soldered a commercially available NFC-chip onto metal pads. Thereby, the board remains flexible and even the small mounting pads adhere under bending stress (Figure [4e](#adfm202412196-fig-0004)). Onto the chip, we stored a website-URL (jku.at), which can be read by any NFC reader, e.g. a smartphone (Video [S1](#support-information-section), Supporting Information). The NFC-chip still remains fully functional and bendable after being stored four months under ambient conditions (≈23 °C, ≈40% r.H.), showing excellent adhesion of all copper traces and solder joints (Video [S2](#support-information-section), Supporting Information).

## 3 Conclusion

We presented chemical modifications of pure mycelium skin and report on methods that enhance its mechanical and electrical properties in favor of its applicability as substrate in sustainable electronics. These processes enable the extraction of valuable chitosan as a by-product and are otherwise harmless for the environment. The resulting mycelium foil exhibits unchanged thermal stability and biodegradability compared to untreated samples, yet a smoothed surface due to structural changes of the hyphae network. Thereby, metallic depositions have increased conductivity and durability. Following, the implementation of a biodegradable shellac coating further smoothed the surface while providing a solution for moisture resistance and recyclability. Non-biodegradable components that are mounted on top are recoverable by dissolving the shellac with ethanol, allowing the mycelium foil to be reused. Additionally, we used shellac as an adhesive layer to laminate and structure copper foils on top of our substrate, enabling flexible NFC-tags with unmatched sustainability. Our innovations in mycelium-based electronics may well initiate the development of scalable and industrially compatible electronics manufacturing processes that foster a greener future.

## 4 Experimental Section

#### Materials

All chemicals were used as received without further purification. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 99.89%; ACM), acetic acid (AcOH, 99.8%+; CL Chemicals), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%; Merck), sulfuric acid (H <sub>2</sub> SO <sub>4</sub>, 95%; VWR Chemicals), ethanol (abs. 100% a.r.; Chem-Lab NV) were used for the chemical treatment of the mycelium skin. Shellac flakes (A.F. Suter & Co.Ltd.) were used to fabricate shellac coatings and adhesion layers.

#### Mycelium Skin Growth and Harvest According to Danninger et al.\[\]

One kilogram of dry beech wood shavings (Räucherspan.de Buche Typ 7) was thoroughly mixed with ≈50 g of organic full-grain spelt flour (local grocery store) and 25 g of fine plaster (CaSO4) dust (local construction market). This mixture together with 2 L of water was boiled in a pressure cooker for at least 30 min for sterilization. The wet substrate was filled in sterilized polypropylene boxes to a height of 4 to 8 cm. After cooling down to room temperature with a closed lid, ≈500 g of beech wood–based inoculum (Tyroler Glückspilze Sägemehlbrut G. lucidum Sopron strain) was evenly distributed to all containers, manually ripped apart into small pieces, and mixed thoroughly under the substrate. As a separation grid, a strong polyethylene fly screen with square holes of 0.5 mm in width was cut to fit onto the surface of the substrate in the containers. After sterilization with boiling water and isopropanol, the fly screen patches were placed on the substrate, the lid were closed tightly to minimize gas exchange, and the boxes were stored in a dark space at ≈25 °C. After appropriate fungus growth, the separation grid was ripped off the solidified substrate together with the mycelium skin. The mycelium skin was peeled off carefully from the separation grid using a blade or a spatula. The wet skin was repeatedly placed between two paper towels and pressed gently to remove most of the water content. When the mycelium skin was dry to the touch, it was placed between two straight wood plates and left to completely dry while being forced flat.

#### Chemical Treatments

Dog bone-shaped (ISO 527-2:2012(E) Type 5A) mycelium samples were fully submerged in heated deionized water, NaOH, AcOH, or HCl within a 500 mL round bottom flask under constant stirring. The flask was placed into a heated water or oil bath for temperature regulation and was attached to a condenser. For deionized water, the samples were treated for 4.5 h at 100 °C. For NaOH, the samples were exposed to the solution for 2 h at 90 °C and then rinsed with a 1:1 water/ethanol mixture to remove any salt residues. For AcOH, the samples were exposed to the solution for 6 h at 60 °C and then rinsed with deionized water. For HCl, the samples were exposed to the solution for 18 h at 105 °C. Contrary to the previous procedures, the treatment with sulfuric acid was carried out in small beakers at room temperature. Here, samples were exposed to the solution until no significant change was visible anymore.

#### Uniaxial Tensile Tests

Mycelium skin specimens were punched out with a dog bone-shaped punching tool based on ISO 527-2:2012(E) type 5A and were tested before and after a chemical treatment with a Zwick Roell Z005 (100 N load cell). The specimens were stored in a climate chamber for a day at 30% r.H. prior to the test ensuring equal testing conditions (*n* = 7, from three different mycelium foils)

#### SEM Measurement

SEM measurements were performed using the Zeiss 1540 XB CrossBeam SEM (acceleration voltage, 5 keV). Before imaging, the samples were covered with ≈10 nm of gold via thermal evaporation (pressure, ≈3 × 10 <sup>−6</sup> mbar; deposition rate, 0.1 to 0.3 As <sup>−1</sup>) to increase surface conductivity and thus image quality.

#### Surface Roughness Measurement

Chemically treated mycelium samples were placed in a profilometer (DektakXT, Bruker) and the surface roughness *R <sub>rms</sub>* was determined by calculating the root mean square average over a scanning length of 1 mm and averaging over all measured traces. (*n* = 100)

#### TGA Measurement

A crucible was filled with ≈5 mg of mycelium skin, placed in a Mettler Toledo TGA, and flooded with pure N <sub>2</sub>. The temperature was set to 25 °C and increased with a rate of 0.166 K s <sup>−1</sup> until 500 °C.

#### Biodecomposition Measurement

The biodecomposition measurement was reproduced according to Danninger et al.<sup>[</sup><sup><a href="#adfm202412196-bib-0028" aria-label="Reference 28 - Sci. Adv.">28</a></sup><sup>]</sup>

#### Surface Metallization (PVD)

Mycelium skin samples were metallized with a PVD system (Oerlikon Leybold Univex 350), using a vacuum pressure of less than 5 × 10 <sup>−6</sup> mbar. The deposition rates were 0.02 nm s <sup>−1</sup> for chromium, 0.3 nm s <sup>−1</sup> for copper.

#### Conductivity of Metalized Mycelium Foil

The conductivity of a metalized trace on a mycelium skin was determined by measuring the resistance with a multimeter (Keithley 2110) in 4-wire configuration. All mycelium samples are metalized with chromium-copper (3 nm Cr, 200 nm Cu) and the desired traces and pads were created by removing the negative by laser-ablation with a fiber laser cutter (Trotec Speedy300 flexx). The conductivity 𝜎 was calculated as following:

where *R* is the measured resistance, *A* the cross-section (1 mm × 200 nm), *l* the length (10 mm) of the copper metal trace. (*n* = 20)

#### Cyclic Bending

A mycelium sample with an engraved trace was placed on a 75 µm thick polyimide foil (Kapton HN). The foil was clamped between two parallel poly(methyl methacrylate) plates and the resistance was determined with a multimeter (Keithley 2611A) in 4-wire configuration. The distance between the two plates was adjusted periodically and the bending radius is approximated as the halved distance between the two plates. The measured trace had a deposited chromium-copper layer (3 nm Cr, 200 nm Cu) with a width of 1 mm and a length of 10 mm.

#### Optical Microscope Investigation

The microscope images were captured with a Nikon Eclipse LV100ND optical microscope.

#### Dielectric Breakdown Measurement

Young mycelium skins were positioned between two brass stamps connected to a high voltage supply (140-35000, FuG Elektonik GmbH). The voltage was continuously increased with 50 Vs <sup>−1</sup> until a breakdown/sharp voltage drop occurred (*n* = 13).

#### Electrical Property Measurement

Double-sided metallized mycelium samples (3 nm Cr, 200 nm Cu) were engraved on both sides to form a plate capacitor with an area of 78.5 mm <sup>2</sup>. The conducting electrodes were engraved in opposite directions and were connected with an impedance analyzer (Novocontrol Alpha A Analyzer). The impedance of the capacitor was determined between 10 mHz an 10 MHz with 1 V <sub>rms</sub> of AC voltage applied. The relative permittivity *ε <sub>r</sub>*, the conductivity *σ* and loss tangent *δ* are calculated from the complex impedance, taking account of the geometry of the electrodes (*n* = 7).

#### Shellac Coating

Mycelium samples were airbrush coated with a 1:4 shellac/ethanol mixture.

#### Moisture Uptake of Mycelium Skin

Mycelium samples were placed in a climate chamber (C-40/350, CTS) and continuously weighed with a precision scale (AEJ, KERN). The humidity was sequentially increased from 10% r.H. To 75% r.H. in 5% steps with a waiting time of 1 h in between. The ambient temperature was continuously set to 23 °C.

#### Electrical Heating Measurement

Shellac-coated mycelium samples were metalized (200 nm Cu) and engraved to yield a trace with a width of 2 mm. The traces were consecutively connected to a lab power supply (GPD-3303D, GW Instek) with step-wise increased current settings until failure. Simultaneously a thermal infrared camera (325sc, FLIR) performs temperature measurements on the mycelium samples.

#### Lamination of Mycelium Skin with Copper Foil

Mycelium samples were one-sided brush coated with a 1:2 shellac/ethanol mixture and covered with a 10 µm copper foil on top of the shellac. To ensure a smooth surface during the evaporation process of the ethanol, the composite is pressed together by poly(tetrafluoroethylene) plates at a temperature of 100 °C.
